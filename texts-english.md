## 2 · Who created the creator?

*Corpus A · monologue, to a friend, over coffee.*

**2.1** Okay, I'm going to tell you why the creator story doesn't convince me.

**2.2** Your argument, if I take it seriously, is: something as complicated as the universe, somebody must have designed it. Fine. But then, this somebody, what's he like? Even more complicated, obviously. To make a universe, you need a huge intelligence. And a huge intelligence doesn't come out of nowhere, does it? So who created him?

**2.3** So now you're going to tell me: "Ah no, God doesn't need a cause. He's always existed." Hang on. Why does he get that pass, and not the universe? My question was: where does this creator come from? You haven't answered that. You've just pushed the problem back one step. Before, we had to explain where the universe comes from. Now we have to explain where God comes from. And then you act as if the problem had gone away.

**2.4** I'll grant you, where the universe comes from, we don't know. Nobody knows, me neither. But saying "I don't know" is honest. Saying "it's God" is piling an even bigger mystery on top of the first one.

**2.5** And the more you think about it, the less it holds up. An explanation is normally supposed to make things simpler. Here you're explaining something complicated with something even more complicated. That's the opposite of an explanation, really.

**2.6** And look at what happened with life. Before Darwin, everyone was sure you needed a designer. An eye, a wing, that couldn't happen on its own. And actually, it could. Bit by bit, no plan, nobody behind it. Now, I'm not saying we know everything about the universe, okay. I'm just saying we've already been wrong once, in exactly the same way. You with me?

*Comprehension cue (the friend's objection):* "Ah no, God doesn't need a cause. He's always existed."

## 6 · The accident of geography

*Corpus A · monologue, to a friend.*

**6.1** Hey, there's something that's been bugging me for a long time. If you'd been born in Saudi Arabia, what would you be? Most likely Muslim. In Brazil? Christian. In India? Hindu. And you, born here, to your parents? Jewish. Obviously.

**6.2** Now picture Rome, two thousand years ago. Everyone believed in Jupiter. They sacrificed animals to him, they prayed to him before a battle. Today, for you, it's ridiculous. But if you'd been born there, you'd have believed in him too, like everyone else. You wouldn't have thought: "No, actually, it's probably the God of the Jews who's right."

**6.3** So religion is less a question of truth than a question of postcode. About eight in ten people in the world keep the religion they grew up with (Pew Research Center, 2025). Not because they checked, but because that's what they got at home, along with the language, the food, the culture and the values.

**6.4** And here's what really gets me. When you think of a Muslim in Egypt, you're certain he's wrong: poor guy, he was raised to believe a lie. And he looks at you exactly the same way. You each feel sorry for the other, and each of you is sure he's the lucky one.

**6.5** So yes, I can see your answer coming: "That proves nothing, maybe one of them is true." True, I'll grant you that. But they can't all be true. So most people on earth are wrong, billions of them, and every single one is sure it's the others who are wrong. The Egyptian is sure it's you. You're sure it's him. So how do you know you're not one of them?

**6.6** And honestly, if God really wanted us to know him, why would he leave it to the accident of birth? Just look at yourself the way you look at him. If you'd been born in Syria, you'd be sitting here defending Islam, just as sure as you are now. Doesn't that bother you a bit?

*Comprehension cue (the friend's objection):* "That proves nothing, maybe one of them is true."

## 8 · What I say when people tell me… (God)

*Monologue (v24): his words only; the friend's objections are quoted by him.*

8.1 You'll say: 'You can't prove God doesn't exist. Admit it.' No, I can't. But actually, it's not up to me to prove it. If I tell you there's a teapot going round Mars, you won't ask me to prove it isn't there. You'll say: 'Show it to me.'

8.2 Then you answer: 'Comparing God to a teapot…' I know, it's a bit much, I'll grant you that. Still, the principle holds. It's the one making the claim who has to show something. And me, honestly, I've never seen anything.

8.3 And then you tell me: 'Look around you! All this didn't happen on its own.' Right there, do you see what you're doing? You start from 'we don't know how it happened', and you jump straight to 'so it's God'. In between, there's missing… well, everything's missing. Me, when I don't know, I say I don't know.

8.4 Next, you tell me: 'Without God, life has no meaning.' It has the meaning you give it. Listen, when your daughter makes you a drawing, it moves you, right? Nobody ordered it from above, and yet it matters.

8.5 You'll tell me that's got nothing to do with it. Yes it does, that's exactly it. Meaning doesn't fall from the sky, we make it. And let me tell you, a meaning that's delivered to you ready-made is worth less than a meaning you've built yourself.

8.6 And death, then? You ask me whether I'm not scared. Yes, of course I am. I'm not going to act tough. But think for two seconds. Before you were born, you weren't there for billions of years, and it didn't bother you. After, it'll be the same.

8.7 You find that chilling? It's chilling, yes. But just because it's chilling doesn't mean it's false. I prefer a truth I don't like to a story that reassures me.

8.8 And then there's the classic argument: 'And if you're wrong? You're risking a lot. Me, if I'm wrong, I lose nothing.' Ah, Pascal's wager. Except, which god did you bet on? Because if it's not the right one, you lose too. There are thousands of them, you know.

8.9 And in the end you always tell me: 'It's not a question of proof. It's faith.' There we go, that's the point. If nothing in the world can make you change your mind, it's not really reasoning any more, it's a choice. And I respect it, really, sincerely. But you mustn't call it proof.

8.10 You'll find me harsh. No, I'm direct, it's not the same. And anyway, we're friends, right? The more honest we are, the more it's worth talking about.

## 10 · The word "colonialism"

*Corpus B · monologue, to a French friend. Steelman first, answer second.*

**10.1** I'm going to start with your argument in its strongest form, because that's the one I want to answer.

**10.2** Your version is: Zionism comes from Europe. It organised immigration, bought land, and in 1948 roughly seven hundred thousand Palestinians left or were driven out. And you know what? All of that is true. Herzl himself talked about colonisation; at the time the word didn't shock anybody. And the Nakba, the people who lost their homes, I don't deny it.

**10.3** So yes, the movement had colonial features, I'll grant you that. But the word "colonialism" means something else. It describes a country sending its people somewhere else, for its own benefit. A colonist has a mother country behind him. The French in Algeria had France.

**10.4** The Jews who arrived didn't have a mother country. They came from countries that didn't want them at all anymore, or worse. And they didn't arrive just anywhere. There have always been Jews in that land; it's where everything begins for them.

**10.5** And there's something people almost never say in France. After 1948, around eight hundred and fifty thousand Jews left Arab and Muslim countries, or were expelled from them. Iraq, Yemen, Libya, Morocco. Today, roughly half of Israeli Jews come from those families.

**10.6** You're going to tell me: "That doesn't erase 1948." No, you're right, it doesn't erase anything. Two injustices don't cancel each other out. But it means we're not talking about Europeans settling in other people's land. We're largely talking about refugees, on both sides.

**10.7** So the real question isn't "were there injustices?" There were. It's: is a people returning to where its roots are the same thing as an empire conquering a distant country? I don't think so. And if you put the same word on both, you stop understanding either story.

## 14 · Did we have a choice?

*Corpus B · monologue, to a French friend. Two legs: stated intent, demonstrated capability. Then the honest seam: this establishes the right to respond, not the manner. → hands off to #15 (not yet written).*

**14.1** People often tell me: "You could have done it differently." Fine. But differently how? Because the choice was never between war and peace.

**14.2** On October 7th, Hamas killed about twelve hundred people, mostly civilians, and took roughly two hundred and fifty hostages. That's the starting point. And what struck me was what they said right afterwards.

**14.3** On October 24th, 2023, Ghazi Hamad, from Hamas's political bureau, goes on LBC, a Lebanese channel. And he says, I'll translate for you: "Al-Aqsa Flood is just the first time. There will be a second, a third, a fourth." In France, when I tell people this, they don't believe me. But it's on film, it's translated, anyone can check.

**14.4** That's the first pillar: the intent, they announced it. The second is the means. Under Gaza, there were tunnels everywhere. How many kilometres? Here you have to be honest: nobody independent has been able to measure. Israeli officials talk about five hundred to seven hundred kilometres. Fine, let's even take half. That's still a city under the city, built with cement that could have been used to build above ground.

**14.5** So look at what we were facing. A force that had shown what it could do, and said it would do it again. Would you leave it in place? That was the real choice. Act, or wait for next time.

**14.6** But hang on, I'll stop there, because I can see your answer coming, and it's a fair one. Everything I've just said shows we had the right to respond. It says nothing about how. The right to go in, and the way you go in, legally, those are two different questions. And mixing them up is the best way to lose this argument.

**14.7** And I'll even give you an argument against myself. Some legal scholars say that self-defence, as the UN Charter provides for it, fits poorly against an armed group coming from a territory you control. It's a real debate; I'm not going to act like it doesn't exist.

**14.8** So, the manner, let's talk about it. It's the hardest question, and it's the one that really matters.

## 17 · What I say when people tell me… (Israel)

*Monologue (v24): his words only; the friend's objections are quoted by him.*

17.1 You'll say: 'Amnesty, Human Rights Watch, and even B'Tselem, an Israeli NGO, all say the same thing.' 'It's an apartheid regime. I'm not the one making it up.' No, I know, and they're not clowns. So I'll start with what's true. In the West Bank, there are two legal systems. A settler goes before a civilian court, a Palestinian before a military court. That can't be defended, and I won't try.

17.2 And you'll conclude: 'So it's apartheid.' That's where you lose me completely. In Israel itself, one inhabitant in five is Arab. They vote, there are Arab members of parliament, there was an Arab party in government in 2021, there's an Arab judge on the Supreme Court. Discrimination? Yes, there is some, real discrimination. But in South Africa, Black people couldn't vote at all.

17.3 You'll tell me the West Bank isn't a detail. No, you're right, it's not a detail. It's an occupation, since 1967, and it's gone on far too long. Except an occupation ends with an agreement. The word 'apartheid' says the country is rotten to the core. And if it's rotten to the core, there's nothing left to negotiate. Are you with me?

17.4 And then there's Gaza. You'll say: 'A UN commission concluded it was genocide. A UN commission, mind you, not a blogger.' I know, the Pillay commission, in September 2025. And I'm not going to play down the deaths. The Gaza health ministry, which answers to Hamas, counts more than seventy thousand. Its figure mixes civilians and fighters. But even starting from the lowest estimates, there are a huge number of civilians. It's terrible, full stop.

17.5 So do I admit it? I acknowledge the deaths. But 'genocide', in law, means something precise: the intention to destroy a people as such. That's the whole question. An atrocious war in the middle of a city, against an enemy hiding under the houses, is not the same thing. Even when the toll is horrific.

17.6 You'll tell me the International Court of Justice did say it was plausible. Oh no, we've read that everywhere, but that's not what it said. The Court's president at the time, Joan Donoghue, explained it herself to the BBC. The Court said the Palestinians had a plausible right to be protected from genocide. Not that there was a plausible genocide. And on the merits, it hasn't ruled yet.

17.7 And Netanyahu, you ask me whether I condemn him, yes or no. I criticise him, and not just a little. There were hundreds of thousands of us in the street, in Israel, against his government. But you see, criticising a government is one thing. Saying a country has no right to exist is another. When you criticise Macron, nobody asks you whether France should disappear.

17.8 And the settlements? You'll tell me they're not legitimate. No. The settlements in the West Bank are an obstacle to peace, and lots of Israelis think like you. But wait, remember Gaza. In 2005, we left. All the settlements dismantled, down to the last one. Two years later, Hamas had taken power. So when people tell me 'leave, and things will get better', fine, I'm willing. But last time, it didn't go as planned.

17.9 You'll tell me I've got an answer for everything. Not at all. There are lots of things I don't know, and others where I disagree with my own side. But I'd rather we talk about the real problems than about words that shut the discussion down. Do you want us to get somewhere, or do you want to be right?

## 1 · L'indifférence du cosmos

*Corpus A · monologue.*

**1.1** You know what made me drop it, deep down? It's not an argument. It's the scale. Look at the sky one night, really look. Hundreds of billions of galaxies, each with billions of stars.

**1.2** And the universe is about fourteen billion years old. If you squeeze it into a single year, humanity shows up on the thirty-first of December, in the last few minutes. And we'd like all that to have been made for us? Honestly, that seems a bit much to me.

**1.3** And then there's suffering. In 2004, a tsunami kills more than two hundred thousand people in one day. Believers, atheists, babies. The wave didn't pick and choose. To me, that doesn't look like a world someone is watching over. It looks like a world that doesn't care.

**1.4** That's what Dawkins says, roughly. In the universe there's no design, no purpose, no good and no evil. Just blind indifference. And what we see is exactly what we'd expect if there were nobody behind it.

**1.5** So yes, I can see your answer coming: 'That's horrible, what you're saying. Living in a world like that is unbearable.' I understand. But just because an idea is frightening doesn't make it false. The world doesn't have to be comfortable.

**1.6** And I'll go even further. If the universe doesn't care, then kindness comes from us. Nobody imposed it on us. When someone helps a stranger, it's not an order from above, it's a choice. I find that more beautiful, not less. Don't you?

## 3 · Le mauvais design

*Corpus A · monologue.*

**3.1** You know what makes me doubt there's really a great engineer behind all this? It's not the beauty of the world. It's the flaws.

**3.2** Take a nerve in your neck, the laryngeal nerve. It goes from the brain to the throat, to make your voice work. The brain and the throat are right next to each other: about ten centimetres. If you were the one designing the body, what would you do? A straight nerve, obviously. Ten centimetres of nerve is plenty. Except this nerve doesn't take that route. It goes all the way down into the chest, as if someone had pulled it down. It loops around a big artery right next to the heart, and comes all the way back up to the throat.

**3.3** In the giraffe, it's even crazier. Its neck is almost two metres long. So the nerve goes all the way down the neck, loops around the artery, and goes all the way back up. A detour of several metres, to end up a few centimetres from where it started. An engineer who did that, you'd fire him. A creator supposedly sent this nerve through the chest to get from the brain to the throat? That's a bit much.

**3.4** Now look at a fish. A fish has no neck. In a fish, the brain, the heart and the gills are close together, and the nerve passes behind the artery with no detour. It's the shortest route. And our very distant ancestors were fish. Over the generations, the neck got longer, the heart moved down into the chest, and the nerve stayed stuck behind the artery. It couldn't switch sides, so it just got longer along with it. The detour is just a trace of our history.

**3.5** With evolution, this detour is fully explained. With an engineer, it isn't explained at all.

**3.6** And it's not the only flaw, you know. There are loads of others: the retina fitted backwards, childbirth being so dangerous, wisdom teeth, back pain. If you're curious, look into it, it's worth it.

**3.7** So yes, I can see your answer coming: 'Maybe God has his reasons.' Fine. But look at what you're doing. When it's well made, it's proof of the creator. When it's badly made, it's the mystery of his reasons. You win every time. And an idea that wins every time explains nothing.

**3.8** Me, I prefer an explanation that predicts the flaws to one that excuses them after the fact. Evolution tinkers with what it has. And what we see everywhere is exactly tinkering. You with me?

## 4 · Les 2 999 autres dieux

*Corpus A · monologue.*

**4.1** Do you believe in Zeus? No. In Thor? No again. Ra, the Egyptians' sun god? Still no. Millions of people believed in him, though. They made sacrifices to him, they built him temples.

**4.2** Humans have worshipped thousands of gods. Ricky Gervais says roughly this: you reject nearly all of them, except one. I just reject one more. So we actually agree on almost everything.

**4.3** And why don't you believe in Thor? Think for two seconds. Because nobody has ever shown anything. Because it's a story people told in one corner of the world, at one time. Well, I take exactly the same reasons and apply them to yours.

**4.4** So yes, you'll tell me: 'My God isn't a bearded guy with a hammer.' And I'll grant you, there's a difference. Philosophers talk about him as a first cause, outside time, outside space. That is something other than Thor, it's true.

**4.5** Except nobody prays to the philosophers' God. The one you pray to chose a people, spoke to prophets, answers when you ask him. And that one looks a lot more like Thor than like a first cause.

**4.6** So I'm asking you. The reasons that make you laugh at Zeus, why wouldn't they work for yours? It's not up to me to explain why I don't believe in one more god. It's up to you to explain why you stop at that one.

## 5 · Si on effaçait tout

*Corpus A · monologue.*

**5.1** Imagine something. Tomorrow, we wipe everything. All the science books, all the holy books, all the memories. And we wait a thousand years.

**5.2** The science books will come back. Not right away, but they'll come back. Someone will drop an apple again, measure, start over. And one day, someone will find exactly the same law of gravity. Same for water boiling at a hundred degrees, same for the atom.

**5.3** Why? Because it's there, outside. It doesn't depend on who's looking. You can find it again anywhere, any time.

**5.4** And the Torah? Will someone find Moses again, Mount Sinai, the ten commandments, word for word? Will someone find Shabbat on Saturday again? Obviously not.

**5.5** So yes, I can see your answer coming: 'But religions would come back too.' And you're right. I'm not saying people would stop believing. Humans have always had gods, rituals, prayers. That would grow back, for sure.

**5.6** But look at the difference. Science would come back the same. Religion would come back, but different. Other gods, other stories, other rules.

**5.7** And what strikes me is this. A truth, normally, you find again. A story, you reinvent. So the real question isn't 'would people still believe?'. It's 'would they believe the same thing?'. And there, we all know the answer, don't we?

## 7 · La morale sans Dieu

*Corpus A · monologue.*

**7.1** People often say to me: 'Without God, why would you be a good person? Everything would be allowed.' Honestly, that question scares me a bit. If the only thing stopping you from killing your neighbour is fear of hell, keep believing, please.

**7.2** Okay, I'm joking. Let's take the question seriously. There's an old problem, it goes back to Plato. Is the good good because God commands it? Or does God command it because it's good?

**7.3** If it's the first answer, the day God commands cruelty, cruelty becomes good. Do you believe that? And if it's the second, then the good exists without him. Either way, God isn't much help to you.

**7.4** And look at what you already do. In the Torah, a man who gathers wood on Shabbat gets stoned. Would you do it? No. Slavery: the Torah regulates it, it doesn't forbid it. Would you defend it? Not that either. So you're already sorting. And that sorting, what do you do it with? With your own morality. It was there before the book.

**7.5** I'll grant you, where morality comes from is a real question, and philosophers still argue about it. But just because it's hard doesn't mean the answer is God. We have empathy, we live in groups, we need to trust each other. To start with, that's plenty.

**7.6** And I'll go even further. A morality that comes from above, you follow out of obedience. A morality you understand, you follow because you've understood why. In someone else, which of the two reassures you more?

## 9 · Le droit d'être là

*Corpus B · monologue.*

**9.1** People often tell me: 'Israel is an artificial country. The Europeans created it to get rid of their Jews.' So let's talk about it, because the other side's argument isn't stupid.

**9.2** In 1917, Britain promises a Jewish national home in Palestine. That's the Balfour Declaration. And at that time, the vast majority of the inhabitants are Arabs. A colonial power promises land that isn't its own, without asking the people who live there. Seen like that, it's shocking, I'll grant you.

**9.3** But look at what happens right after. The Ottoman Empire collapses, and the whole Middle East is carved up again. In 1920, at San Remo, the victors share out the mandates. France takes Syria and Lebanon. Britain takes Iraq and Palestine.

**9.4** In 1922, the League of Nations confirms all that. And the text of the mandate speaks, in black and white, of the Jewish people's historical connection with this land. Out of that come Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Jordan. And Israel.

**9.5** So if that process is illegitimate, it's illegitimate for everyone. Why would Israel be artificial, and not Jordan? Nobody asks whether Lebanon has the right to exist.

**9.6** And besides, the Jews didn't fall from the sky in 1948. There have always been some there, in Jerusalem, Safed, Hebron, Tiberias. For centuries. The mass return starts in the 1880s, well before the Holocaust. And by the end of the nineteenth century, Jews were already the largest group in Jerusalem.

**9.7** Then, in 1947, the UN votes for partition. A Jewish state, an Arab state. It's an international vote, as for other countries born of decolonisation.

**9.8** Now careful, I'm not saying that settles everything. It says nothing about borders, nothing about the West Bank, nothing about the refugees. Those are other discussions, and they're hard ones. I'm just saying Israel's right to exist is no more fragile than its neighbours'. And that, I won't let go of.

## 11 · Ce qu'on a proposé, ce qui a été refusé

*Corpus B · monologue.*

**11.1** You often hear: 'Israel never wanted a Palestinian state.' So let's run through the history, quickly. And I'll start with the other side's argument, because it's serious.

**11.2** In 1947, the UN proposes partitioning the country. Jews are about a third of the population, and they're given more than half the land. Okay, a good part of it is the Negev desert, but still. Seen from the other side, why say yes to that? I understand why they refused. Really.

**11.3** But look at what came next. The Jewish side accepts. The Arab side refuses, and the neighbouring armies attack. And ten years earlier, in 1937, it was already the same story with the Peel plan. The Jews were offered a small piece of territory, about a fifth. The Zionist leaders argued about the borders, but they accepted the principle. The Arab leaders said no straight away.

**11.4** In 2000, at Camp David, Barak offers a state. There, to be honest, the offer is disputed. The Palestinians say it was badly carved up, and too vague on Jerusalem. Let's grant that. But a few months later, Clinton puts his own parameters on the table. Israel says yes, with reservations. Arafat too, but with so many reservations it amounted to a no.

**11.5** And in 2008, Olmert goes even further. About ninety-four percent of the West Bank, plus land swaps to make up the rest. Jerusalem's holy sites under international management. Abbas never answered. Years later, he said it himself on Israeli TV: he refused.

**11.6** Now careful, I'm not saying it's all their fault. The settlements are a real problem, and we'll talk about them. I'm just saying the line 'Israel never wanted peace' doesn't hold. What we did wrong is one thing. What we offered is another.

**11.7** Every time there was a partition plan, one side said yes. And it was always the same one. Do you see it differently?

## 12 · On est partis de Gaza

*Corpus B · monologue.*

**12.1** People often tell me: 'The problem is the occupation. Leave, and the violence will stop.' I understand the idea. Except we tried that.

**12.2** In 2005, Israel leaves Gaza. Every settlement, without exception. About twenty villages, roughly eight thousand residents. Soldiers pulled Israeli families out of their homes, sometimes crying with them. Even the dead were dug up to be taken along. And the army left, down to the last soldier.

**12.3** And then? In 2006, Hamas wins the elections. In 2007, it takes power by force, throwing Fatah people off buildings. And the rockets didn't go down. They went up. Thousands, on Sderot, on Ashkelon.

**12.4** Now I'll give you the other side's argument, because it's serious. Israel still controlled the airspace, the sea, most of the crossings. For the UN, Gaza remained an occupied territory. And the blockade made life there very hard. All of that is true.

**12.5** But look at the order of things. The blockade comes after Hamas took power, not before. And in 2005, there were plans. Greenhouses bought by donors, so the economy could restart. Some of them were looted in the days that followed.

**12.6** So I'm not saying Gaza was free. I'm just saying the idea 'leave, and things will calm down' was tested for real. With a total withdrawal, not a symbolic gesture. And the result wasn't peace.

**12.7** So when people talk to me about leaving the West Bank, I listen. Really. But you understand why many Israelis answer: 'We've already given.' In their place, would you do it again with your eyes closed?

## 13 · Le 7 octobre

*Corpus B · monologue.*

**13.1** When we talk about October 7th, people often tell me: 'Yes, but in Gaza too, civilians are dying. It's the same.' And I understand why people say that. But no, it's not the same, and I'll explain why.

**13.2** First, the facts. About twelve hundred dead in one day. Roughly two thirds civilians, including thirty-six children. More than two hundred and fifty hostages, from babies to old people. At the Nova festival, about three hundred and sixty young people killed in a few hours.

**13.3** In the kibbutzim, they went in house by house. They looked for people in the shelters. The civilians weren't collateral damage. They were the target.

**13.4** And what struck me is that they filmed it. Themselves. With cameras on their heads, with the victims' phones. There's even a recording where one of the attackers calls his parents, proud, to tell them how many Jews he's killed.

**13.5** So why does intent matter? Take a car accident. A guy who kills someone because he braked badly, and a guy who drives into a crowd on purpose. The dead person is the same. But nobody says it's the same crime.

**13.6** In the law of war, it's the same. Targeting civilians on purpose is a war crime, full stop. Killing civilians while aiming at a military target is judged differently: was it necessary, was it proportionate. And there, yes, you can criticise Israel, strike by strike.

**13.7** I'll even give you two arguments against me. The first, the UN Secretary-General said it: it didn't come out of nowhere. The blockade, the occupation, all that is true. But explaining is one thing. Excusing is another.

**13.8** The second: that day, some Israelis died under Israeli fire, in the chaos of the fighting. The army acknowledged it, it investigated. That's true, and it's terrible. But it doesn't change who came, and why.

**13.9** So yes, the dead in Gaza count. I say it without hesitating. But when someone puts the two on the same level, I just ask him one thing. The bad driver and the one who drives into the crowd on purpose: do you think that's the same?

## 15 · Pourquoi il y a tant de morts à Gaza

*Corpus B · monologue.*

**15.1** Before I say anything, I want to start here. The dead in Gaza are real, and it's terrible. Children, whole families. I'm not going to do sums to make it look less serious. It is serious.

**15.2** You know the figure. Gaza's health ministry says more than seventy thousand dead. It's a Hamas ministry, yes. But in the past, its figures have mostly held up, so I start from there.

**15.3** Except that ministry doesn't distinguish between civilians and fighters. And there, the numbers go all over the place. The Israeli army says it killed about twenty thousand fighters. A Guardian investigation, based on the army's own database, found far fewer. So I'm not going to tell you they were all terrorists. That would be false.

**15.4** But the question I'm asked is: does that number prove a crime? And there, 'proportionality', in law, means something precise. It's not a ratio between your dead and mine. It's, for each strike: is the expected harm to civilians excessive relative to the anticipated military advantage?

**15.5** Strike by strike. So the total shows there's been a tragedy. On its own, it says nothing about the question of a crime.

**15.6** So why so many dead? Look at how Hamas fights. Tunnels under houses, rocket launchers next to schools, fighters without uniforms. In law, that's called human shields, and it's a war crime. And it's no accident. In 2024, the Wall Street Journal published messages from Sinwar. He talked about civilian deaths as 'necessary sacrifices'.

**15.7** I see you coming: 'Human shields don't entitle you to fire anyway.' You're right. The other side cheating doesn't remove your obligations. And there are strikes that raise real questions. The seven World Central Kitchen aid workers, in 2024: the army itself admitted a grave error. I don't defend everything.

**15.8** Now, compare with other urban wars. In Mosul, against ISIS, between nine and eleven thousand civilians died, according to AP. In Raqqa, the coalition killed about sixteen hundred, according to Amnesty. I'm not saying it's the same. Gaza is longer, denser, with many more dead. I'm just saying urban war, against an enemy hiding among civilians, is atrocious everywhere.

**15.9** So my position isn't 'everything's fine'. The number of dead is a tragedy. Whether it's a crime is judged strike by strike. And whoever put his fighters under the hospitals carries a share of those deaths. Not all of it. A share.

## 18 · Les ultra-orthodoxes, l'armée et l'argent

*Monologue (v24): his words only; the friend's objections are quoted by him.*

18.1 You're asking me why the ultra-Orthodox don't do the army in Israel. It's an old story. In 1948, Ben-Gurion exempts four hundred yeshiva students. Four hundred. After the Holocaust, the idea was to save a world that had almost vanished. That, I can understand.

18.2 Except four hundred was in 1948. Today, the Haredim are about one Israeli in seven. And we're talking about roughly eighty thousand young men of service age who don't serve.

18.3 You'll say: 'For them, studying Torah also protects the country. It's their way of serving.' I know, that's their argument, and for a believer it's not nothing. But look at what it gives in reality. Since October 7th, reservists have done hundreds of days. Fathers who left their jobs and their kids. If study really protected us, why is it always the same people who go and get killed?

18.4 They're also afraid the army will change their sons, make them secular. That, I can hear. But the army has made an effort. There are units with no women, strict kashrut, prayer times. There's even a whole brigade just for them. So 'the army will change them' is an argument that holds less and less.

18.5 And you'll say: 'And Israeli Arabs? They don't serve either. Why go after the Haredim only?' That's a really good question. The difference is we never wanted to send Arabs to fight against people of their own nation. It's the state that excused them; they didn't negotiate it. But I'll grant you, if I talk about equal duties, it applies to them too. A civilian service, for example, for everyone.

18.6 And what does the law say? In June 2024, the Supreme Court said, unanimously: there's no legal basis for this exemption, they have to be called up. The first year, the army sent about twenty-four thousand call-up orders. Know how many were enlisted? Fewer than a thousand.

18.7 Then there's money. You'll tell me they pay taxes like everyone else. Right, here you have to be precise, because this is where people talk nonsense. They're not exempt from taxes. They pay VAT like everyone. The problem is that among the men, about one in two doesn't work. Among other Jewish men, it's over eighty percent who work. Haredi women work almost as much as other women. It's the men who are the problem.

18.8 And what does that mean, concretely? It means they're nearly one in seven, and they pay about two percent of income tax. The Bank of Israel did the maths, household by household. A non-Haredi Jewish household gives the state, on average, about six thousand shekels a month more than it gets back. A Haredi household gets about four thousand more than it gives.

18.9 You'll say: 'But they have six, seven kids, they're poor.' 'A poor family, back home too, gets more than it pays. That's normal.' You're right on one point, and their defenders say it too. In these calculations, every child counts: school, health care. A family with seven children costs more, it's mechanical. So part of the gap is just family size.

18.10 But only part of it. But not all, and that's the heart of the problem. A poor family, I'm happy to help. But this poverty doesn't fall from the sky. To keep the exemption, you have to stay in yeshiva. The day you take a job, you can be called up. So the state pays people not to work, and then it's surprised they don't work.

18.11 And do the ones who study get a fortune? No, and I won't exaggerate. A married man studying in kollel gets a few hundred shekels a month. Nobody gets rich on that. The money goes through child benefits, daycare, local-tax discounts. It's not a scam, it's a system. And it's the system I'm criticising.

18.12 You'll say: 'It's their religion. You can't ask them to betray the Torah.' Ah, the Torah, let's talk about it. Do you know Maimonides, the Rambam? The greatest codifier of Jewish law. Not some secular guy from Tel Aviv, mind you. He writes that whoever decides to study without working, and to live off charity, desecrates God's name. He disgraces the Torah. He puts out the light of religion.

18.13 Word for word, or nearly. And in Pirkei Avot there's this image: don't make the Torah a spade to dig with. A spade, meaning a livelihood. And he himself was a doctor. He worked all day, and studied afterwards.

18.14 You'll tell me other rabbis say the opposite. True, I'll grant you that. Rabbi Yosef Karo, the Shulchan Aruch one, answers him that times have changed, that it's fine to support those who study. It's a real debate between rabbis. But I'm not saying the Torah forbids study. I'm just saying the greatest of them found it shameful to live off it.

18.15 So what do I actually want? I don't want them to stop studying. I want them to share. The real question isn't 'do we respect the Torah?'. It's 'are there two kinds of citizens?'. And I'd rather have a country where everyone pulls their weight than one where some carry the weight of others.
